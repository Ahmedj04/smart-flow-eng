Automation Health Check for UK Manufacturing Reliability

A practical guide to identifying ageing controls, drive systems, electrical risks and maintenance gaps before they become avoidable production problems.

5 October 2026 Smart Flow Engineering 9 min read

Industrial manufacturing depends on reliable automation. PLCs, HMIs, variable speed drives, motors, control panels, networks and electrical systems all have a role in keeping production moving. When one of those systems becomes unreliable or unsupported, the resulting problem can be far larger than a single component failure.

That is why an industrial automation health check can be valuable for UK manufacturers. Instead of waiting for a breakdown, a structured assessment looks at the condition, supportability and obsolescence risk of critical automation and electrical assets, then turns the findings into prioritised actions.

The key point: an automation health check does not promise that a factory will never experience downtime. Its purpose is to identify avoidable risks early, improve maintenance decisions and create a practical upgrade roadmap.

What is an industrial automation health check?

An industrial automation health check is a structured review of the equipment and systems that support production. Depending on the site, this can include PLCs, HMIs, industrial drives, motors, control panels, electrical distribution, I/O, communications, machine interfaces and supporting documentation.

The assessment considers more than whether a machine is running today. A healthy-looking system may still contain an obsolete PLC, an unsupported HMI, a drive that is difficult to source, missing backups or a single point of failure that could create a lengthy recovery period after a fault.

For UK manufacturing businesses, the most useful output is therefore not simply a list of defects. It is a clear view of condition, criticality, obsolescence, maintainability and recommended next steps.

Why downtime risk starts before the machine stops

Production downtime is often treated as the moment when a machine stops. From an engineering perspective, the risk can begin much earlier.

An ageing automation system may continue operating for years while support options become narrower. Spare parts may take longer to source. Engineering knowledge may be concentrated in one person. Software backups may be incomplete. A replacement component may no longer be a direct substitute. Each of these conditions can increase the impact of a future fault.

Common warning signs

  • PLC, HMI or drive hardware is approaching the end of manufacturer support.
  • Critical spare parts are difficult to source or have long lead times.
  • Control-system software or PLC programmes are not backed up and version-controlled.
  • Repeated drive, motor, electrical or control faults are being treated as isolated breakdowns.
  • Panel drawings, I/O lists or machine documentation are incomplete or out of date.
  • Only one engineer or contractor understands a critical legacy system.
  • Planned maintenance is focused on reacting to failures rather than prioritising critical assets.

How an automation health check identifies obsolescence risk

Automation obsolescence is not simply about equipment being old. A component becomes a meaningful business risk when its age, support status, availability, condition or integration with the wider system makes recovery increasingly difficult.

A health check can map critical assets against their current condition and supportability. This creates an evidence-based starting point for deciding what should be monitored, maintained, refurbished, stocked as a critical spare or upgraded.

1. PLC and HMI obsolescence

PLCs and HMIs are central to many automated production systems. During an assessment, engineers can review the installed platforms, hardware condition, software availability, backups, communications and practical replacement options.

A PLC obsolescence assessment is particularly useful where a plant has legacy controllers that still operate reliably but may have limited support or replacement availability. The right response is not automatically to replace every old PLC. The objective is to identify which assets create material operational risk and which can continue to be supported safely and economically.

2. Drives and motor-control risk

Variable speed drives and motor-control equipment are frequent points of attention because they combine electrical, electronic and software dependencies. A drive may be operational while its cooling, connections, configuration, spare availability or support position needs review.

Reviewing drives alongside motors, applications and control architecture can help identify where a future failure could affect a critical process. Where an upgrade is justified, the work can be planned around production constraints rather than initiated by an emergency breakdown.

For a deeper look at this area, see our Industrial Drives and Motion services.

3. Electrical and control-panel condition

Automation reliability is closely connected to the electrical infrastructure supporting it. Connections, protection, panel condition, power quality considerations, field wiring and equipment environment can all influence reliability.

A practical assessment should therefore consider the wider system rather than looking at the PLC or drive in isolation. Our Electrical & MEICA services support this broader engineering view.

How a health check can reduce avoidable downtime

The value of an automation health check for manufacturing comes from turning technical observations into decisions. A typical process can be structured around five stages:

Stage What is reviewed Typical output
Assess Condition, age, criticality and supportability Asset and risk baseline
Identify Failure points, recurring faults and obsolescence exposure Prioritised risk list
Maintain Inspection, backups, critical spares and preventive tasks Maintenance actions
Upgrade Legacy drives, PLCs, HMIs and electrical/control equipment Upgrade options and roadmap
Commission Testing, functional checks and return to production Controlled implementation and handover

This approach helps separate urgent risks from longer-term improvements. It also allows maintenance and capital expenditure to be planned around the operational importance of each asset.

Why preventive maintenance matters in UK manufacturing

Maintenance is not only a productivity issue. The UK Health and Safety Executive states that work equipment must be maintained in an efficient state, in efficient working order and in good repair under PUWER Regulation 5. HSE guidance also discusses planned preventive and condition-based maintenance as approaches for managing equipment risks.

Read the HSE Approved Code of Practice and guidance for PUWER for the relevant maintenance guidance.

For manufacturers, this reinforces an important principle: maintenance should be planned around the condition, use and risk of the equipment. HSE guidance notes that maintenance frequency should take account of factors such as intensity of use, operating environment, variety of operations and risk from failure.

From reactive maintenance to planned intervention

A health check provides the information needed to move from a purely reactive model towards targeted maintenance. Instead of treating every asset equally, a manufacturer can focus engineering time on equipment where deterioration or failure would have the greatest operational consequence.

Our preventive maintenance and technical support services are designed around this principle, including electrical inspections, drive checks, panel inspections, fault investigation, PLC backups and shutdown support.

What should a manufacturing automation health check include?

The exact scope should reflect the plant and its critical processes, but a useful automation health check UK programme can include:

  1. Asset identification: record critical automation, electrical and control assets.
  2. Condition review: identify visible condition issues, recurring faults and maintenance concerns.
  3. Obsolescence review: identify legacy or unsupported equipment and parts availability concerns.
  4. Control-system review: consider PLCs, HMIs, I/O, networks, backups and control architecture.
  5. Drive and motor review: assess critical drive applications, condition and supportability.
  6. Electrical review: consider panels, connections, protection and relevant equipment condition.
  7. Documentation review: check drawings, programmes, manuals, settings and records where available.
  8. Risk prioritisation: classify findings by operational importance and practical urgency.
  9. Action planning: define maintenance, spare, monitoring and upgrade recommendations.

When should a UK manufacturer consider a health check?

There is no single age at which every factory should replace its automation systems. A health check becomes particularly useful when a business notices increasing faults, ageing equipment, support concerns or a major production change.

Before a major production upgrade

When a new machine or process is being introduced, reviewing the existing automation architecture can reveal compatibility, capacity and obsolescence issues before installation work begins. This can reduce the risk of discovering a legacy constraint during commissioning.

Before planned shutdowns

A shutdown creates an opportunity to complete work that would be difficult during production. A condition and obsolescence review can help decide which maintenance or upgrade activities deserve priority during that window.

After repeated breakdowns

If the same equipment repeatedly causes production interruptions, replacing individual failed components may not address the underlying risk. A broader review can identify whether the problem is condition, environment, control architecture, documentation, maintenance or obsolescence.

When critical equipment is becoming unsupported

If a manufacturer, PLC, HMI or drive platform is becoming difficult to support, early planning gives the business more options. An upgrade can then be designed, tested and commissioned in a controlled way rather than being forced by an unexpected failure.

From assessment to upgrade: making the findings actionable

The strongest automation health check is not a report that sits on a shelf. It should lead to decisions. Smart Flow Engineering follows an Assess. Maintain. Upgrade. Integrate. Commission. approach so findings can be connected to practical engineering work.

Depending on the findings, the next step may be a preventive maintenance activity, a critical-spares strategy, a PLC or HMI upgrade, a drive replacement, control-panel modification, electrical work or a wider automation integration project.

Where an upgrade is required, testing and commissioning provide the controlled path from installation through functional testing, start-up and handover.

Why an evidence-led approach is better than replacing everything

Legacy equipment is not automatically a problem. Replacing every older component can introduce unnecessary cost, engineering change and production disruption.

An evidence-led industrial automation and electrical engineering programme asks better questions: Is the asset critical? Is it reliable? Can it be supported? Are spares available? Is the software recoverable? What happens if it fails? Can the risk be managed through maintenance, or is an upgrade justified?

This makes the health check a decision-making tool rather than simply an equipment inspection.
